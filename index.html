Document 1: Address of the Colored State Convention to the People of South Carolina

Charleston, South Carolina — November 24, 1865. Delegates of formerly enslaved and free Black South Carolinians, gathered five months after the war's end, speaking directly to the state's white citizens.

Select text, then click a color to highlight your evidence.

Passage 1 — Who is speaking, and why
We have assembled as delegates representing the colored people of the State of South Carolina, in the capacity of State Convention, to confer together and to deliberate upon our intellectual, moral, industrial, civil, and political condition as affected by the great changes which have taken place in this State.
Guiding question: What change gives these delegates the standing to speak this way, in public, to the whole state? What does the act of holding this convention itself tell you — achieved or still in progress?

Passage 2 — What they're asking for
We ask for no special privileges, or peculiar favors. We ask only for even-handed justice — for the removal of such positive obstructions and disabilities as past and recent legislation has thrown in our way and heaped upon us.
Guiding question: The delegates are careful to say they want "even-handed justice," not "special privileges." Why might that distinction have mattered to their audience? Is the goal here achieved or blocked, and what lens fits?

Passage 3 — What's still blocked
We... have, with few exceptions, been virtually excluded from the rights of citizenship, which you cheerfully accord to strangers, notwithstanding we have been born and reared in your midst. We are denied the right of giving our testimony in the courts of the State, in consequence of which our persons and property are subject to every species of violence and insult, and fraud and spoliation, without redress.

Document 2: President Andrew Johnson's Veto of the Freedmen's Bureau Bill

Washington, D.C. — February 19, 1866. Johnson's message returning to Congress a bill that would have extended and strengthened the Freedmen's Bureau.

Highlight the same way as above.

Passage 1 — No necessity claim
There is no immediate necessity for the proposed measure. The act to establish a bureau for the relief of freedmen and refugees, which was approved in the month of March last, has not yet expired. It was thought stringent and extensive enough for the purpose in view in time of war.
Guiding question: Johnson says a stronger Bureau bill isn't necessary. Whose interests does this argument serve? From the perspective of formerly enslaved people, is this passage evidence of something achieved or something blocked? Tag the lens.
Write your own answer first — the check button unlocks after a few sentences.
Passage 2 — Cost and scale objection
The appointment of an agent for every county and parish will create an immense patronage; and the expense of the numerous officers and their clerks, to be appointed by the President, will be great in the beginning, with a tendency steadily to increase.
Guiding question: What specific goal does this objection block, and for whom? Would a formerly enslaved person reading this in 1866 see it as a fair, neutral concern — or something else? Explain, and tag it.
Write your own answer first — the check button unlocks after a few sentences.
Passage 3 — Trust in the state courts
With the Federal courts restored and those of the several States in the full exercise of their functions, the rights and interests of all classes of people will, with the aid of the military in cases of resistance to the laws, be essentially protected against unconstitutional infringement or violation.
Guiding question: Johnson claims the state courts, once "restored," will protect everyone's rights. Compare this directly to Document 1, Passage 3 — what did the Convention delegates say about those same state courts? Mark this passage and tag the lens.
